The Roads That Cross Over Water

The first way over moving water was a tree. You pushed one down across the water and walked over it. That tree was doing something harder than it looks, and understanding what makes it hard is most of the story.

Take anything long and hold it up at its two ends only. Now put something heavy in the middle. It will start to give: the top of it pressed together, the bottom of it pulled the other way. Wood and rock are very strong when they are pressed together and much less strong when they are pulled. So the tree breaks along the bottom first, and it breaks in the middle.

People have found three good answers to this, and all three are still used today.

The first is to build a rounded way up and over out of pieces of rock, high in the middle and coming down to the ground at both ends. Every piece in it is pressed by the pieces beside it and by all the pieces above. Nothing in the whole thing is ever pulled. Rock loves to be pressed. Some built this way have been carrying people for two hundred years and more, and no one has had to do a thing to them.

The second is to fill the space under the road with wood or hard stuff from the ground, set as three sided forms, one after another in a line. A three sided form can not change without one of its three pieces breaking. Press on a four sided form and it falls over into a long low one. Press on a three sided form and nothing moves at all. Something heavy in the middle is carried out to the ends, a little of it through each piece.

The third is to hang the road from above. Two tall standing parts are built up out of the water, and long lines of hard stuff run over their tops and down to the ground far away at each end, where they are held fast. From those long lines, shorter lines drop straight down and hold up the road. Every line is only ever pulled, never pressed, and being pulled is the one thing a line is very good at. This way you can cross water so wide that nothing at all could be built in the middle of it.

All three do the same thing in the end. Nothing is ever really held up. The press of the road, and of everyone on it, is only passed along from piece to piece until it reaches the ground at either end.
