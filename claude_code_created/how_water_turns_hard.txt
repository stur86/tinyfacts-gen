How Water Turns Hard

Water is made of pieces far too small to see. In warm water those pieces are moving fast, running past each other, turning, never holding on to anything for long. That is why water runs, and why it takes the form of any cup you put it in.

Make the water colder and they slow down. Cold is not a thing that pushes. Cold is only what is left when the moving stops. As the pieces slow, they begin to feel the small pull that each one has for the ones beside it. In the end they are moving too slowly to break free. They take hold, and they set into lines.

Once they are in lines they can not run past each other any more. They can only shake in place. The water has become hard. We call it ice.

Here is the strange part. Almost everything in the world gets smaller as it gets colder. Water does not. The lines the pieces fall into hold them a little farther from each other than they were when they were running free. So the same water takes up more room after it turns hard, and it is lighter than it was.

That is why ice sits on top of water instead of falling to the bottom. Think what would happen if it did not. Every deep cold water in the world would fill up with ice from the bottom, year after year, until there was nothing else in it, and everything living there would die. Because the ice stays on top, it lies over the water like a cover, and under that cover the water stays warm enough for life to go on.

It is also why a full bottle left outside in the cold will crack. The water inside grows as it turns hard, and there is no room for it to go.

And every one of the small white bits that fall from the sky in the cold months has six sides. Not five, not seven. Six, every time, because six is the way those tiny pieces take hold of each other. No two of them are ever quite the same, and yet every one has the six.
