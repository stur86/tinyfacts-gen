How The Eye Sees

Your eye is a small ball full of water. It is also the best thing of its kind in the world, and no one built it.

Light comes off everything around you, off the wall, off a chair, off the face of a friend, and a very small part of that light happens to go into your eye through the dark round opening in the front. That opening grows wider when the room is dark and smaller when the light is strong, so that not too much and not too little gets in.

Behind the opening sits a clear round piece, like glass. As the light goes through it, it is turned in toward the middle. All the light coming from one spot in the room is turned so that it lands on one spot at the back of the eye. Light from another spot lands somewhere else. What you get on the back wall of the eye is a picture of the room: small, and, strange as it sounds, the wrong way up.

That back wall is covered with more tiny cells than anyone could ever name. When light falls on one of them, it sends a small push along a line running back to the brain. Some of these cells answer to very low light but can not tell one color from another, which is why at night everything looks grey. The others need more light, and each one answers to red, or to green, or to blue. Only three answers. But put the three together in every way you can and out comes every color you have ever seen.

The brain does the rest. It turns the picture the right way up. It takes the two pictures from your two eyes and makes them one, and because the two are not quite the same, it works out how far away everything is.

There is one place at the back of each eye where the line to the brain leaves. No cells can sit there, so there is a small piece missing from what you see. You have never once noticed it. Your brain quietly fills it in with whatever it thinks should be there.

That is a good thing to remember when you believe you are looking at the world. You are looking at something your brain has built, out of the little light that got in.
